# Parsimonious Generative Machine Learning for Non-Gaussian Tail Modeling and Risk-Neutral Distribution Extraction

Qi Wu<sup>a</sup>, Zhonghao Xian<sup>a</sup>, Xing Yan<sup>b,*</sup>, Nan Yang<sup>b</sup>

*<sup>a</sup>School of Data Science, City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong SAR*
*<sup>b</sup>Institute of Statistics and Big Data, Renmin University of China, Beijing, China*

## Abstract

In financial modeling problems, non-Gaussian tails exist widely in many circumstances. Among them, the accurate estimation of risk-neutral distribution (RND) from option prices is of great importance for researchers and practitioners. A precise RND can provide valuable information regarding the market’s expectations, and can further help empirical asset pricing studies. This paper presents a parsimonious parametric approach to extract RNDs of underlying asset returns by using a generative machine learning model. The model incorporates the asymmetric heavy tails property of returns with a clever design. To calibrate the model, we design a Monte Carlo algorithm that has good capability with the assistance of modern machine learning computing tools. Numerically, the model fits Heston option prices well and captures the main shapes of implied volatility curves. Empirically, using S&P 500 index option prices, we demonstrate that the model outperforms some popular parametric density methods under mean absolute error. Furthermore, the skewness and kurtosis of RNDs extracted by our model are consistent with intuitive expectations. More generally, the proposed methodology is widely applicable in data fitting and probabilistic forecasting.

*Keywords:* Risk-Neutral Distribution, Non-Gaussian Tails, Generative Machine Learning, No Arbitrage, Calibration, Implied Volatility Curves

# 1. **Introduction**

It has been extensively documented that non-Gaussian tails exist widely in financial markets (<font color="blue">Rachev, 2003</font>; <font color="blue">Jondeau et al., 2007</font>). Moreover, generally, the tails are asymmetric on the left and the right sides (<font color="blue">Cont, 2001</font>; <font color="blue">McNeil et al., 2015</font>). To avoid the concern of using Gaussian assumption, many advanced distributions were developed, such as the normal inverse Gaussian distribution (<font color="blue">Barndorff-Nielsen, 1997</font>), the hyperbolic distribution (<font color="blue">Barndorff-Nielsen, 1977</font>), the variance gamma distribution (<font color="blue">Madan and Seneta, 1990</font>), the generalized skewed $t$ distribution (<font color="blue">Theodossiou, 1998</font>), and so on. Most of them are defined via a relatively complicated density function, which restricts the flexible usage in numerical applications. While the advantage is the analytical tractability, the disadvantages are still significant, such as the inconvenient sampling, the difficult computation of the expectation of a function of the random variable, the further optimization of it, and the optimization of the conditional densities.

Derivatives are very versatile instruments and options are a big family. Option pricing and related topics have long been an active and crucial research area. In mathematical finance, researchers usually make a set of assumptions about what stochastic processes the return and the volatility follow. It ends with a parametric model that takes some market signals as the inputs, then outputs the corresponding option price. The seminal work Black-Scholes-Merton model in <font color="blue">Black and Scholes (1973)</font> initiated modern derivatives pricing theories by giving theoretical prices for vanilla European options, by taking the Gaussian assumption. Since then, many studies have been carried out on finding better option pricing models that allow more complex distributions for asset returns, such as the Heston model in <font color="blue">Heston (1993)</font> which is widely used now.

Besides, the direct extraction of the risk-neutral distribution (RND), which is the distribution of the future underlying asset price/return in the risk-neutral world, is of great importance for both researchers and market practitioners. Theoretically, option prices reflect information about the financial market, represent the market's expectations on the future, and provide a path for investments and risk management. The temporal behavior of the second moment of the market index's RND is a barometer for monitoring the systemic risk or is the proxy of a risk factor. More specifically,

Parametric models assume functional forms that provide economic interpretation for their parameters. Semi-parametric methods are less popular because they are more complicated mathematically. We aim to recover the risk-neutral distribution of the underlying asset return in the real financial market with the consideration of asymmetric non-Gaussian tails. The probability of extreme loss is higher than the probability estimated with the Gaussian assumption, which is the main reason for the volatility smiles after the market crash of 1987. Therefore, the key to the problem is to allow asymmetrically non-Gaussian tail behavior in the model. We successfully achieve this and show the merits of the proposed method by extensive numerical results in this paper.

## 2. **Background and Motivation**

There were many distributions proposed in financial literature that depart from Gaussian. Some of them aim at modeling the real-world or physical distributions, such as the ones mentioned in the beginning of Section <font color="blue">1</font>, while some others are used in risk-neutral worlds. This paper focuses on the risk-neutral distribution, but the methodology here is general and is applicable in both of the two worlds.

### 2.1. *Some Existing Methods for RND Extraction*

It is first necessary to specify the form of the risk-neutral distribution. Then the estimation/calibration of the distribution must be done given the market option prices. Many forms of distributions have been proposed in the previous literature, and we choose three popular ones to show below.

*Double Log-Normal Distribution (DLN).* <font color="blue">Bahra (1996)</font>, <font color="blue">Söderlind and Svensson (1997)</font>, and <font color="blue">Melick and Thomas (1997)</font> were the first three describing the risk-neutral distribution with a mixture of distributions. A mixture of densities is a convenient way to extend the flexibility, to better describe the data statistically. For the options, the mixture of log-normal densities is a well-known distribution for the reason that it contains the single log-normal and can be viewed as an extension of the Black-Scholes-Merton model. The

5

---



log-normal density with mean $\mu$ and volatility $\sigma$ is defined by:

$$l(s_T; \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{s_T \sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\log(s_T) - \mu}{\sigma} \right)^2 \right).$$

The mixture of two such densities yields:

$$q(s_T; \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{2} \alpha_i l(s_T; \mu_i, \sigma_i).$$

Here $q(s_T; \theta)$, where $\theta = \{\mu_i, \sigma_i, \alpha_i, i = 1, 2\}$, is used as the risk-neutral density function of the underlying asset price $S_T$ at time $T$.

*Generalized Beta Density (GB).* <font color="blue">Bookstaber and McDonald (1987)</font> proposed the generalized beta distribution to model the risk-neutral distribution. Denote that $a > 0$ is the power parameter, $b > 0$ is the scale parameter, $v > 0$ is the first beta parameter, and $w > 0$ is the second beta parameter. Let $B$ be a beta random variable with parameters $v$ and $w$, then $X = b(B/(1-B))^{1/a}$ is a generalized beta with parameters $(a, b, v, w)$. For the complete density function which is much complicated, please refer to <font color="blue">Bookstaber and McDonald (1987)</font>; <font color="blue">Jondeau et al. (2007)</font>.

*Edgeworth Expansion (EW).* As mentioned in <font color="blue">Jondeau et al. (2007)</font>, <font color="blue">Jarrow and Rudd (1982)</font> captured deviations from log-normality by an Edgeworth expansion of the risk-neutral distribution around the log-normal distribution. Letting $Q$ be the $cdf$ of the price random variable $S_T$, <font color="blue">Jarrow and Rudd (1982)</font> showed that an Edgeworth expansion in densities for the true probability distribution $Q$ around the log-normal $cdf$ $L$ can be written up to order 4 with cumulants. With some assumptions, the price of a European call option with strike price $K$ can be approximated. The implied volatility, skewness, and kurtosis can be estimated with nonlinear least squares using this expression.

*Other nonparametric or semi-parametric methods.* There were several nonparametric or semi-parametric methods proposed in the literature. <font color="blue">Monteiro and Santos (2022)</font> proposed a nonparametric kernel function approach with efforts of dealing with convexity, monotonicity, and no-arbitrage. <font color="blue">Abadir and Rockinger (2003)</font> and <font color="blue">Bu and Hadri (2007)</font> proposed the semi-parametric density functionals based on confluent hypergeometric functions (DFCH). For more methods, please also refer to the introduction section. For comparisons among various methods, please refer to <font color="blue">Santos and Guerra (2015)</font>; <font color="blue">Lu (2019)</font>;

<font color="blue">Ammann and Feser (2019)</font>. As we have argued in the introduction section, parametric methods for modeling RND directly have merits such as the tail properties inherently incorporated, the ease of calibration/recovering, and the ease of computing risk-neutral distributional characteristics/moments, so we mainly consider parametric methods in this paper.

## 2.2. *Generative Machine Learning*

Recent successful generative machine learning (GML) models embrace a completely different paradigm for modeling a distribution. The density function or distributional function is not the objective anymore. Instead, a new methodology of latent variable transformation is adopted. To be specific, a latent random vector or variable $Z$ having a commonly-known distribution (e.g., Gaussian) is assumed, and the target variable(s) $Y$ of interest (e.g., the observations in a dataset) is assumed to be generated by

$$ Y = F_{\Theta}(Z), \tag{1} $$

where $F_{\Theta}$ is usually a deep neural network that needs to be learned to make $Y$ distributed closely to the observed data. In different generative models, there are different choices for $F_{\Theta}$ and different learning strategies for $\Theta$. The most common ones include Variational Autoencoder (VAE) (<font color="blue">Kingma and Welling, 2014</font>), Generative Adversarial Net (GAN) (<font color="blue">Goodfellow et al., 2014</font>), Normalizing Flow (NF) (<font color="blue">Papamakarios et al., 2021</font>), and their plentiful variants.

The generative models mentioned above have made great achievements in modeling and generating image data, time series, and so on. They all use a deep neural network as $F_{\Theta}$ since the data is usually high-dimensional and exhibits a low-dimensional manifold structure. Such a premise may not be the case in this paper. In a financial market which is a highly risk-sensitive world, modeling a distribution needs an elaborate description of how it is apart from Gaussian, such as on the tail behavior or the asymmetry. <font color="blue">Wu and Yan (2019)</font> proposed a non-Gaussian distribution in the form of a quantile function for Value-at-Risk (VaR) prediction in financial markets. It is equivalent to choosing a standard Gaussian-distributed $Z$ in Equation (<font color="blue">1</font>) and carefully designing an $F_{\Theta}$ to make $Y$ have asymmetric left and right non-Gaussian tails. We adopt such an idea in this paper for the choice of risk-neutral distribution, since it has some unique merits as we will see in the rest of the paper.

7

---



An important general merit of such generative models over traditional density-based distribution modeling approaches is the convenience for sampling. Despite the lack of a closed-form density function, it is very easy to sample from Equation (1) by generating a realization of $Z$ and feeding it into $F_{\Theta}$. This is extremely useful in the financial world, where we may need Monte Carlo simulation throughout, especially in derivatives markets. The density-based sampling undoubtedly brings difficulties. Some arguments state that the density-based approach is easier to do the estimation with the maximum likelihood. By choosing $F_{\Theta}$ as a bijection, the density of $F_{\Theta}(Z)$ can be easily computed numerically and the maximum likelihood estimation can be conducted too. Moreover, when $Z$ is univariate and $F_{\Theta}$ is strictly increasing, the estimation can be done via quantile regression, which is effective and stable, as shown in <font color="blue">Wu and Yan (2019)</font>.

Besides, the density-based approach suffers in the optimization, because many densities with asymmetric non-Gaussian tails are complicated in mathematical forms. When estimating conditional distributions, or when computing and optimizing the expectation of a function of the random variable, the complicated densities will show their shortcomings. In this paper, we are confident about learning a succinct and effective latent-based generative model with merits for risk-neural distribution extraction. Meanwhile, risk-neutral moments or distributional characteristics such as volatility, skewness, and kurtosis can be easily computed through simulating with Equation (1).

## 3. **Parsimonious Generative Machine Learning**

It is commonly known that asset returns in both physical worlds and risk-neutral worlds exhibit asymmetry and non-Gaussian tails. The impact of asymmetry and non-Gaussian tails of RND on the shape of the implied volatility curve is intuitive. Given the fact that real implied volatility curves may exhibit smile, skew, or slope, it is natural to design a distribution family with asymmetry and non-Gaussian tails for RND. This is also useful and meaningful for modeling distributions in physical worlds.

### 3.1. *An Existing Parsimonious GML Model*

First proposed by <font color="blue">Wu and Yan (2019)</font> and further modified by <font color="blue">Yan et al. (2019)</font>, researchers proposed a novel parametric quantile function to represent a univariate distribution for asset returns in the physical world. It is a

8

---



monotonically increasing nonlinear transformation of the standard Gaussian quantile function $\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)$, $\alpha \in (0, 1)$:

$$Q(\alpha \mid \mu, \sigma, u, v) = \mu + \sigma \Phi^{-1}(\alpha) \left( \frac{u^{\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)}}{A} + \frac{v^{-\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)}}{A} + 1 \right), \tag{2}$$

where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are location and scale parameters, and $A (= 4)$ is a hyperparameter to normalize the distribution. The resulting quantile function $Q(\alpha \mid \mu, \sigma, u, v)$ has (heavier) non-Gaussian right and left tails controlled by the parameters $u \ge 1$ and $v \ge 1$. We can observe a reversed S shape in the Q-Q plot of $Q(\alpha \mid \mu, \sigma, u, v)$ against $\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)$. Specifically, the parameter $u$ controls the up tail of the inverted S shape, representing the right tail of the corresponding distribution, while the parameter $v$ controls the down tail, representing the left tail of the corresponding distribution. The larger they are, the heavier the tails are. Setting $u$ or $v$ to 1 results in an exactly Gaussian tail.

We demonstrate the impact of these two parameters on the density function of $Q(\alpha \mid \mu, \sigma, u, v)$ in <u>Figure 1</u>, with the solid line representing the normal distribution when $u = v = 1.0$ for reference. We obtain a right heavier tail than Gaussian by setting $u = 5.0$ and $v = 1.0$, and a left heavier tail than Gaussian by setting $u = 1.0$ and $v = 4.0$. Due to the monotonic property of the transformation on $\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)$, the inverse of $Q(\alpha \mid \mu, \sigma, u, v)$ is uniquely determined and is a distributional function. Also because of the monotonic property, this methodology is equivalent to applying the same transformation:

$$Y = \mu + \sigma Z \left( \frac{u^Z}{A} + \frac{v^{-Z}}{A} + 1 \right), \tag{3}$$

to the standard Gaussian variable $Z \sim N(0, 1)$. The random variable $Y$ has exactly the quantile function $Q(\alpha \mid \mu, \sigma, u, v)$. Because the transformation in <font color="blue">Equation (3)</font> is strictly increasing, the density of $Y$ can be easily computed numerically, and hence the parameter estimation (unconditional or conditional) given observed data can be done by either maximum likelihood or quantile regression (using <font color="blue">Equation (2)</font>).

To begin with, we illustrate our definition of relative tail heaviness.

Given a cumulative distribution function $F$, we denote its survive function $1 - F(x)$ as $\bar{F}(x)$. We always assume $F$ is continuous and strictly increasing in an infinite interval, thus the quantile function $F^{-1}$ exists and is also continuous and strictly increasing. We also denote $f(x) \sim g(x)$ if $\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x)/g(x)$ is a positive constant and $\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = \lim_{x \to \infty} g(x) = 0$ or $\infty$. \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{\bar{F}_2(x)}{\bar{F}_1(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma})} = +\infty, \quad \forall \mu \in \mathbb{R} \text{ and } \sigma > 0. \eqno(4)

*Definition 2.* We also say two distributions have the same left/right tail heaviness if there exists $\mu \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\sigma > 0$ such that $F_1((x - \mu)/\sigma) \sim F_2(x)$ as $x \to -\infty$ or $\bar{F}_1((x - \mu)/\sigma) \sim \bar{F}_2(x)$ as $x \to +\infty$.

The first definition says that tail heaviness means $\bar{F}_2$ goes to 0 more slowly than any location-scale transformation of $\bar{F}_1$, which is consistent with the location-scale transformation of the corresponding random variable. The second definition is an extension, whose intuitive examples are that all normal distributions share the same tail heaviness, all exponential distributions share the same tail heaviness, and all distributions with a fixed tail index share the same tail heaviness. It is easy to show that this definition of equivalent tail heaviness is an equivalence relation in algebraic. The proof is straightforward and is omitted.

**Proposition 1.** *The equivalent tail heaviness in Definition 2 is an equivalence relation on the set of all distributions that are continuous and have non-zero density on the left (or right) tail side.*

### 3.2.1. A General Form of Transformation

The way we picture a new distribution in this work is very different from traditional methods. Briefly, we construct a totally new distribution with arbitrary tail heaviness by designating its quantile function through a transformation of a known base quantile function (equivalently, through random variable transformation). We start from the general idea and introduce our method as generally as possible, with some specific examples followed.

Firstly, we propose a general form of transformation as follows.

*Definition 3.* We define a function $y = f(x)$, $x \in \mathbb{R}$ described as follows to curve the straight line $y = \mu + \sigma x$ in the coordinate plane of $(x, y)$:

$$f(x) = \mu + \sigma x (g_1(x) + g_2(x) + 1) , \eqno(5)$$

where $\sigma > 0$, and $g_1(x), g_2(x)$ satisfy

1. both are differentiable at $x \neq 0$ and continuous at $x \in \mathbb{R}$,
2. $g_1(x)$ is monotonically non-decreasing and $g_2(x)$ is monotonically non-increasing,
3. $\lim_{x \to -\infty} g_1(x) = 0$ and $\lim_{x \to +\infty} g_2(x) = 0$.

11

---

In this definition, it is obvious that $g_i(x) \ge 0, \forall x \in \mathbb{R}, i = 1, 2$. So $f(x)$ approaches $-\infty/+\infty$ as $x$ tends to $-\infty/+\infty$. Moreover, $f(x)$ is differentiable over $(-\infty, +\infty)$. At $x = 0, f'(0) = \lim_{x \to 0} \sigma(g_1(x) + g_2(x) + 1) = \sigma(g_1(0) + g_2(0) + 1)$. Next we prove the monotonic property of $f(x)$. If $f(x)$ is strictly increasing, then $f^{-1}(x)$ uniquely exists on $(-\infty, +\infty)$ and is differentiable.

**Proposition 2.** *If $g_1(x)$ and $g_2(x)$ satisfy the three conditions in Definition 3 and an additional condition: $g_i(x) + xg'_i(x) > -1/2, \forall x \neq 0, i = 1, 2$, then $f(x)$ in Equation (5) is strictly increasing.*

*Proof.* It can be proved from $f'(x) = \sigma(g_1(x) + g_2(x) + 1) + \sigma x(g'_1(x) + g'_2(x)) > 0$ at $x \neq 0$ and $f'(0) = \sigma(g_1(0) + g_2(0) + 1) > 0$. $\square$

This additional condition can be easily satisfied, and thus flexible choices for $g_i(x)$ are available. For example, for $g_1(x)$, the inequality $g_1(x) + xg'_1(x) \ge 0 > -1/2$ always holds when $x > 0$. As to $x < 0$, one can choose a $g_1(x)$ that has small $g'_1(x)$ around 0 and satisfies $\lim_{x \to -\infty} xg'_1(x) = 0$. A simple choice for $g_1(x)$ is $g_1(x) = \mathbb{I}_{\{x \ge 0\}} x^u, u > 1$. $g_2(x)$ can be defined symmetrically, with a different parameter $u$. Intuitively, $y = f(x)$ curves the straight line $y = \mu + \sigma x$ and forms an inverted S shape with fattened tails, while $g_1(x)$ and $g_2(x)$ acting on the upper and lower side respectively. Next, we apply this transformation on a base random variable and equivalently on its quantile function.

**Proposition 3.** *Suppose $F(x)$ is the cumulative distribution function of a random variable $X$ and its inverse $F^{-1}(\alpha), \alpha \in (0, 1)$ is the quantile function. If $f(x)$ satisfies the four conditions in Proposition 2, then the new function $f(F^{-1}(\alpha))$ is the quantile function of the new random variable $Y = f(X)$, i.e., its inverse $F(f^{-1}(x))$ exists and is the cumulative distribution function of $Y = f(X)$.*

*Proof.* It is straightforward according to the three properties of $f(x)$: strictly increasing, differentiable, and approaching $-\infty/+\infty$ over $(-\infty, +\infty)$. $\square$

This leads to an easy simulation property of our method, which is a great advantage compared to the traditional heavy tail modeling approach. To simulate from the distribution of $Y$, we only need to sample from a simpler base distribution of $X$ and then apply the transformation $f$. The transformation $f(x) = x(g_1(x) + 1)$ is applied on $F^{-1}(\alpha)$, and different values for $u$ from the set $\{1, 2, 3\}$ are specified. The comparison among the generated new distributions is shown in Figure 4, where the baseline distribution itself is included too, which is the special case when $g_1(x) \equiv 0, x \in \mathbb{R}$. From the comparison, we can conclude that the transformation $f$ can yield a new distribution with a heavier tail, whose heaviness is controlled by the parameter $u$. Additional to the empirical analysis here, we rigorously give some theoretical results in the following.

3.2.3. *Tail Heaviness*

The tail heaviness of the distribution we construct relies on the tail heaviness of the baseline distribution as well as on $g_1(x)$ and $g_2(x)$ we choose. Due to the similarity, we only need to consider the single side case when $x \to +\infty$ and examine how the asymptotic behavior of $g_1(x)$ affects the right tail heaviness of the new distribution because $g_2(x) \to 0$. For convenience, we assume the cumulative function of the baseline distribution $F(x) < 1$ for any large $x$. We always assume $f(x)$ satisfies the four conditions in <font color="blue">Proposition 2</font>. First, we start with some specific cases. All proofs of them are in the appendix.

**Proposition 4.** *Suppose that the base distribution has the right tail index $\nu$, or equally there exists a positive $\nu$ such that $\lim_{x \to +\infty} x^\nu \bar{F}(x) = c > 0$. If $\lim_{x \to +\infty} x^{-\nu'} g_1(x) = c' > 0$ for some positive $\nu'$, then the distribution determined by the quantile function $f(F^{-1}(\alpha))$ has the right tail index $\nu/(1+\nu')$. Alternatively, if $\lim_{x \to +\infty} e^{-tx^{\nu'}} g_1(x) = c' > 0$ for some $t > 0$ and $\nu' > 0$, then the distribution determined by $f(F^{-1}(\alpha))$ will have a right tail that is as heavy as $(\log x)^{-\nu/\nu'}$.*

Many distribution families accord with the above description of $F(x)$, such as Student's $t$ and Pareto distribution. Through the transformation $f$, a new distribution $F(f^{-1}(x))$ with more flexible tails is born. However, there are other commonly-used benchmark distributions such as the normal which satisfies $\lim_{x \to +\infty} x e^{x^2/2} \bar{F}(x) = c > 0$ and the exponential which satisfies $\lim_{x \to +\infty} e^{tx} \bar{F}(x) = c > 0$. For this situation, we have the following statement.

**Proposition 5.** *(i) Suppose the distribution function $F$ has a Gaussian tail, i.e., $\bar{F}(x) \sim x^{-1} e^{-x^2/2}$ as $x \to +\infty$, and we choose a $g_1(x)$ satisfying that $g_1(x) \sim x^{\nu-1} e^{\nu x^2/2}$ for some $\nu > 0$ as $x \to +\infty$, then the distribution determined by $f(F^{-1}(\alpha))$ has the tail index $1/\nu$. (ii) Suppose $F$ has a exponential tail, i.e., $\exists t > 0$ s.t. $\bar{F}(x) \sim e^{-tx}$ as $x \to +\infty$, and we choose a $g_1(x)$ satisfying that $g_1(x) \sim x^{-1} e^{\nu x}$ for some $\nu > 0$ as $x \to +\infty$, then the distribution determined by $f(F^{-1}(\alpha))$ has the tail index $t/\nu$.*

The above are some special cases of the choices for base distribution $F(x)$ and transformation $f(x)$ (especially $g_1(x)$). Next, we show two general conclusions about the choices of $F(x)$ and $f(x)$, and the tail heaviness of the new distribution after transformation.

16

---



**Proposition 6.** *Suppose $F$ is the baseline cumulative function and $\lim_{x \to +\infty} g_1(x) = +\infty$ in $f(x)$. If $F$ satisfies either one of the following two conditions:*
*   *a) $\exists \rho < 0$ s.t. $\forall t > 0$, $\lim_{x \to +\infty} \bar{F}(tx)/\bar{F}(x) = t^\rho$, or equally, $\bar{F}$ is regularly varying but not slowly varying,*
*   *b) $\exists t > 1$, s.t. $\lim_{x \to +\infty} \bar{F}(tx)/\bar{F}(x) = 0$,*
*then the new distribution determined by the quantile function $f(F^{-1}(\alpha))$ has a heavier right tail than $F$.*

*Remark 1.* The distribution obtained in Equation <u>(2)</u> or <u>(3)</u> has a heavier right tail than Gaussian, because the cumulative function of Gaussian satisfies Condition b) and $u^x/A \to +\infty$. The left-tail side will have a similar conclusion. On the other hand, for any $n \ge 1$, the $n$-th moment of this distribution exists because it is not difficult to verify the existence of $E[Z^n(u^Z/A + v^{-Z}/A + 1)^n]$ when $Z$ is the standard Gaussian. Hence, the tails of this distribution are not so heavy.

Notice that plenty of distributions satisfy either of the two conditions in Proposition <u>6</u>, and thus this proposition says that for many choices of $F$, any transform $f(x)$ satisfying $g_1(x) \to +\infty$ will yield a new distribution with a heavier tail. Furthermore, we state that by choosing appropriate $g_1(x)$ and $g_2(x)$, our proposed transformation can generate a new distribution $F_2$ whose tail is almost arbitrarily heavier than that of the base distribution $F_1$.

**Proposition 7.** *If $F_1, F_2$ are two cumulative functions and $F_2$ has a heavier right tail than $F_1$, i.e., $\lim_{x \to +\infty} \bar{F}_2(x)/\bar{F}_1((x-\mu)/\sigma) = +\infty, \forall \mu \in R, \sigma > 0$. We further assume $F_2$ has a decreasing density function $p_2$ over large $x$ and $F_2^{-1}(\alpha)/F_1^{-1}(\alpha)$ is non-decreasing when $\alpha \to 1^-$. Then there must exists $g_1(x)$ and $g_2(x)$ such that $f(x)$ in Equation <u>(5)</u> makes the distribution determined by the quantile function $f(F_1^{-1}(\alpha))$ have the same right tail heaviness as $F_2$.*

Detailed proofs of these propositions are provided in the appendix.

## 4. Risk-Neural Distribution Extraction

### 4.1. Problem Setup

To formulate the RND extraction problem, we introduce the following notations. Let $t$ be the present time, i.e., the time at which we observe the market, and $T$ be the time at which the options expire (so $T > t$). We denote the spot price of the underlying security as $S_t$ and the price at maturity date

17

---



as $S_T$. The payoff function for a European call option at expiry date $T$ is $V_C(S_T) = \max(S_T - K^C, 0)$, where $K^C$ is the strike price of the call with the superscript $C$ for call and $P$ for put. The payoff function for a European put is $V_P(S_T) = \max(K^P - S_T, 0). <font color="blue">Cox and Ross (1976)</font> showed that in a market with no-arbitrage, the current equilibrium prices of the European call and put options are given by

$$C_t = \tilde{E}[e^{-r(T-t)}V_C(S_T)] = e^{-r(T-t)} \int_{0}^{+\infty} V_C(s_T)f_T(s_T)ds_T,$$

$$P_t = \tilde{E}[e^{-r(T-t)}V_P(S_T)] = e^{-r(T-t)} \int_{0}^{+\infty} V_P(s_T)f_T(s_T)ds_T, \tag{6}$$

where $\tilde{E}$ is the risk-neutral expectation and $f_T(\cdot)$ is the risk-neutral density function of $S_T$. $r$ is the risk-free interest rate. We then denote the log return of the underlying asset as $R_{t:T} := \log(S_T/S_t)$, simplified as $R_\tau$ where $\tau = T-t$ is the time to maturity. The above Equation <font color="blue">(6)</font> can be further written as

$$C_t = \tilde{E} \left[ e^{-r\tau} S_t \left( e^{R_\tau} - \frac{K^C}{S_t} \right)^+ \right] = e^{-r\tau} S_t \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \left( e^{r\tau} - \frac{K^C}{S_t} \right)^+ q_\tau(r_\tau) dr_\tau,$$

$$P_t = \tilde{E} \left[ e^{-r\tau} S_t \left( \frac{K^P}{S_t} - e^{R_\tau} \right)^+ \right] = e^{-r\tau} S_t \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \left( \frac{K^P}{S_t} - e^{r\tau} \right)^+ q_\tau(r_\tau) dr_\tau, \tag{7}$$

where $(\cdot)^+ = \max(\cdot, 0)$ is the positive part function. $q_\tau(\cdot)$ is the risk-neutral density function of $R_\tau$. Now consider we have a model for expressing $q_\tau(\cdot)$ with unknown parameters that need to be calibrated or learned, and we are given $N = N_C + N_P$ options ($N_C$ calls and $N_P$ puts) with observed market prices. The main goal of RND extraction here is to obtain an estimation of $q_\tau(\cdot)$ (in any form, such as density function or GML) by minimizing the following objective:

$$\min \frac{1}{N_C} \sum_{i=1}^{N_C} w_{c_i} L(C_i, \hat{C}_i) + \frac{1}{N_P} \sum_{i=1}^{N_P} w_{p_i} L(P_i, \hat{P}_i), \tag{8}$$

where $L(\cdot, \cdot)$ is the loss function (e.g., MSE or relative-type MSE). $\hat{C}_i$ and $\hat{P}_i$ are option prices estimated by the model, and $C_i$ and $P_i$ are the option prices observed in the market. And $w_{c_i}$ or $w_{p_i}$ is a weight that may be related to the liquidity of the option. Or we may choose equal weights for

18

---

simplicity. Once we have extracted RND, we can price any other path-independent derivatives or further investigate the risk-neutral distributional characteristics or high-order moments which may play an important role in understanding the market’s view of future returns.

## 4.2. *Monte Carlo Pricing with No-Aribtrage Condition*

Instead of describing RND with a density function, we set the form of the underlying asset return using the method described in Section 3. Although there are some available model choices in Section 3, we still adopt Equation (3) because it is concise enough, and its effectiveness has been verified before. Therefore, our model for RND extraction is given by

$$R_{\tau} = \mu_{\tau} + \sigma_{\tau} Z \left( \frac{u_{\tau}^{Z}}{A} + \frac{v_{\tau}^{-Z}}{A} + 1 \right), \eqno(9)$$

where $R_{\tau}$ is the underlying asset return in the risk-neutral world, and $\mu_{\tau}$, $\sigma_{\tau}$, $u_{\tau}$, and $v_{\tau}$ are parameters to be calibrated. For simplicity, we drop the subscript $\tau$ for $\mu, \sigma, u, v$ sometimes in the rest of the paper. We refer to this method as GML, short for generative machine learning.

Given the parameters $\mu, \sigma, u, v$, we can price options with Monte Carlo simulations under the lack of the density for $R_{\tau}$. For European call and put options with contractual parameters (strike prices $K^{C}$, $K^{P}$, the time to maturity $\tau$, interest rate $r$, and underlying price $S_{t}$), we can price them according to Equation (7) by:

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{C}_{t} &= \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} e^{-r\tau} S_{t} \left( e^{R_{\tau,m}} - \frac{K^{C}}{S_{t}} \right)^{+}, \\ \hat{P}_{t} &= \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} e^{-r\tau} S_{t} \left( \frac{K^{P}}{S_{t}} - e^{R_{\tau,m}} \right)^{+}, \end{aligned} \eqno(10)$$

where $R_{\tau,m}$ is the $m$-th *i.i.d.* realization of $R_{\tau}$:

$$R_{\tau,m} = \mu + \sigma Z_{m} \left( \frac{u^{Z_{m}}}{A} + \frac{v^{-Z_{m}}}{A} + 1 \right). \eqno(11)$$

$Z_{1}, Z_{2}, \dots, Z_{M}$ are $M$ *i.i.d.* realizations of standard Gaussian random variable. This pricing strategy provides a promising approximation than the density-based or integral-based pricing approach when the density function in Equation (7) is sophisticated or cannot be written in an explicit form.

19


---



Generally the following condition is to guarantee there is no arbitrage opportunity. When the strike price $K^C = 0$ for call option, i.e., the payoff is $V_C(S_T) = \max(S_T - 0, 0) = S_T$, the call option price should be $S_t$. If this condition is expressed in the density form:

$$S_t = e^{-r\tau} \int_{0}^{+\infty} s_T f_T(s_T) ds_T = e^{-r\tau} S_t \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{r\tau} q_\tau(r_\tau) dr_\tau, \tag{12}$$

it would make this condition difficult to satisfy if the density is in a complicated form. However, from Equation <font color="blue">(10)</font>, this condition in our GML pricing method becomes

$$S_t = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} e^{-r\tau} S_t e^{R_{\tau, m}}. \tag{13}$$

Obviously, this will imply an equivalent condition that the four RND parameters $\mu, \sigma, u, v$ should satisfy:

$$\mu = r \cdot \tau - \log \left( \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} e^{\sigma Z_m} \left( \frac{u Z_m}{A} + \frac{v^{-Z_m}}{A} + 1 \right) \right). \tag{14}$$

This condition informs us that we only need to calibrate the there parameters $\sigma, u, v$ when extracting RND from option prices, and the remaining parameter $\mu$ would be hereafter determined by the above no-arbitrage condition.

### 4.2.1. *Cross-Maturity Arbitrage*

An option's value will decline over time. Or equivalently, the longer the time-to-maturity $\tau$, the greater the option value. Mathematically, the partial derivative of the option value with respect to $\tau$ should be positive. Or, there will be arbitrage opportunity existing. This requires that the RNDs for different $\tau$ satisfy some constraint.

To address it, we may need to calibrate the RNDs for different $\tau$ together, for example, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and so on. Given the fact that the option prices quoted in the market are monotonically increasing with respect to $\tau$ almost all the time, we thereby take a simpler approach of post-processing here. Firstly, we separately calibrate the RNDs for different $\tau$. Then, we examine if the cross-maturity arbitrage is absent, or, if the option values priced with our RNDs are monotonically increasing with those $\tau$. If not, we tune or adjust the RND parameters $\sigma_\tau, u_\tau, v_\tau$ ($\mu_\tau$ is determined by

20

---



them) with a slight amount, to eliminate the cross-maturity arbitrage. More specifically, we increase $\sigma_{\tau_2}$ slightly when the extracted RND at $\tau_2$ yields smaller option values than the RND at $\tau_1$ does, when $\tau_2 > \tau_1$.

Indeed, in our numerical studies, separately calibrating the RNDs for different $\tau$ does not result in the violation of the cross-maturity arbitrage constraint. In other words, the post-processing does not happen even once in our experimental studies. However, the interpolation of RNDs for all $\tau \in [0, T]$ will be a difficult task, and is out of the scope of this paper.

## 4.3. *Calibration of GML Model*

In this paper, we only consider calibrating from European option prices. Calibration is always a critical problem which aims to minimize the gap between estimated prices and real option prices. Suppose we can obtain market prices of $N_C$ call options with strikes $K_i^C$, $i = 1, \ldots, N_C$ and $N_P$ put options with strikes $K_i^P$, $i = 1, \ldots, N_P$ with the same maturity time, denoted as $C_i$ and $P_i$. We minimize the following relative loss between the prices generated by the GML model and the real prices:

$$\min_{\sigma, u, v} L = \frac{1}{N_C} \sum_{i=1}^{N_C} (\hat{C}_i / C_i - 1)^2 + \frac{1}{N_P} \sum_{i=1}^{N_P} (\hat{P}_i / P_i - 1)^2, \tag{15}$$

where $\hat{C}_i, \hat{P}_i$ are the estimated prices using our GML model, see Equation <font color="blue">(10)</font>.

To be more specific, we have designed a detailed algorithm to calibrate our model parameters. Firstly, we fix a random seed to simulate $M = 1,000,000$ samples $Z_m$ from standard Gaussian distribution and initialize the three parameters as $\sigma = 0.2, u = v = 1.1$, followed by Equation <font color="blue">(14)</font> to compute the parameter $\mu$. Then we use Equation <font color="blue">(11)</font> to compute the simulated log-return $R_{\tau, m}$, and compute the estimated option prices by Equation <font color="blue">(10)</font>. The relative mean square error loss $L$ (abbreviated as RLOS) in Equation <font color="blue">(15)</font> is then optimized by Adam optimizer (<font color="blue">Kingma and Ba, 2014</font>) in PyTorch (<font color="blue">Paszke et al., 2019</font>) with the learning rate $\gamma = 0.1$ in every gradient descent step. These steps will be run until the loss no longer decreases with some criteria, called the early-stopping strategy. The algorithm will return to us the calibrated parameters $\sigma$, $u$, $v$, and $\mu$. And we now can express the risk-neutral distribution by Equation <font color="blue">(9)</font> and can evaluate any distributional characteristics of it. We summarize the calibration algorithm details in Algorithm <font color="blue">1</font>.

For instance, it can be used to validate research hypotheses empirically, analyze market expectations, invest and hedge, and price other derivatives. The main advantage of our proposed pricing procedure is that it offers flexibility in capturing different degrees of tailedness in log-return distribution, by succinctly adjusting tail parameters $u$ and $v$. Additionally, our model is convenient for sampling, and easy for computing density values numerically (derived from the quantile function). So, it is beneficial for various numerical purposes.

# 5.2.2. *Calibration with Empirical Data*

We design our empirical experiment as follows. We train the GML model and obtain the calibrated parameters $\hat{\mu}, \hat{\sigma}, \hat{u}, \hat{v}$ for each option maturity by <font color="blue">Algorithm 1</font>. We then evaluate our model's performance using the relative mean square error (relative loss, or RLOS) between the estimated and the observed prices in <font color="blue">Equation (15)</font> as our metric, which is the optimization objective of the calibration. We compare the proposed method with three classical parametric methods for RND extraction, which are DLN, GB, and EW. We calibrate these models' parameters respectively with the R package <font color="blue">RND (<font color="blue">Hamidieh, 2017</font>)</font>. The results are shown in Table 2, in which we report the relative loss averaged annually and consider the options in three groups respectively. According to the days-to-maturity $d$, the three groups are: $d \ge 0$ (all options), $d \ge 14$ (greater than two weeks), and $d \le 365$ (less than one year), which are symbolized with the subscript 0, 14, 365 respectively. We design these categories since some of the options may have fluctuating prices when approaching the expiry or are much illiquid when they are far from expiry.

It is important to note that all models here have very few parameters that need to be calibrated, hence are unlikely to be overfitted. Overall, the GML model outperforms the other three competitors with lower relative losses in all the three days-to-maturity categories in every year of the four. The GB and EW methods for extracting the risk-neutral density sometimes perform poorly, yielding at least ten times the loss of our model or DLN. In the financial crisis year of 2008, our model even achieves superior performance, while the DLN model's loss increases dramatically in that year. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of the GML model and the algorithm in extracting the risk-neutral density from real financial market data, even under extreme market conditions.

# 6. Conclusions

In this paper, we propose to use a new parametric method to extract risk-neutral density of the asset return, which is a type of parsimonious generative machine learning. It has a flexible design with a succinct mathematical form, which can effectively learn asymmetric non-Gaussian tails in the data. We then derive the arbitrage-free conditions it should satisfy in pricing. We also design a Monte Carlo algorithm for calibrating the parameters. With the help of modern machine learning computing tools (such as <font color="blue">PyTorch</font> and <font color="blue">Adam</font>), the algorithm works well on various calibration tasks, as illustrate in the experiments.

We demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach in both self-calibration and fitting Heston option prices in simulation experiments. In real-data empirical studies, we achieve superior calibration results with lower relative losses compared to three classical parametric baselines. We obtain low MAE most of the time when modeling the implied volatility curves and show that our model can capture the main shape and tails of the curve. We proceed to investigate the skewness and kurtosis of RNDs extracted by our model. The results are highly intuitive, such as the kurtosis spike and the skewness spike during the financial crisis in 2008. Overall, our proposed approach offers a powerful and flexible framework for modeling risk-neutral density and it may have further potential applications for both researchers and practitioners.

More generally, the proposed methodology is widely applicable in data fitting and probabilistic forecasting. It is more practical than many existing methods. The parameter estimation or learning given the dataset can be done via maximum likelihood or quantile regression.

### For Proposition 7:

*Proof.* We first prove that when $x \to +\infty$, $F_2^{-1}(F_1(x))/x \to +\infty$, which is equivalent to $\lim_{\alpha \to 1^-} F_2^{-1}(\alpha)/F_1^{-1}(\alpha) = +\infty$. If not, there must exists $M > 0$ and a sequence $x_i \to +\infty$ such that $F_2^{-1}(F_1(x_i)) \leq Mx_i$. So, $F_1(x_i) \leq F_2(Mx_i)$ and $\bar{F}_1(x_i) \geq \bar{F}_2(Mx_i)$, which is contradictory to $\bar{F}_2(Mx)/\bar{F}_1(x) \to +\infty$. Additionally, $F_2^{-1}(F_1(x))/x$ is non-decreasing obviously for large $x$.

We set $g_1(x) = (-\mu + F_2^{-1}(F_1(x))) / (\sigma x) - 1$ for large $x$, where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are any chosen parameters in the formulation of $f(x)$. So $g_1(x) \to +\infty$ as $x \to +\infty$. We further set $g_2(x)$ to be a quickly decaying function satisfying $\lim_{x \to +\infty} xg_2(x) = 0$ and $\lim_{x \to +\infty} xg_2(x) \cdot p_2(h(x))/\bar{F}_2(h(x)) = 0$, where $h(x) = F_2^{-1}(F_1(x))$ (e.g., we can set $g_2(x) \equiv 0$ for large $x$). It is not difficult to let $g_1(x)$ and $g_2(x)$ satisfy all the conditions in Proposition 2. Now,

$$ \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{1 - F_2(x)}{1 - F_1(f^{-1}(x))} = \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{\bar{F}_2(f(x))}{\bar{F}_1(x)} = \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{\bar{F}_2(\sigma x g_2(x) + h(x))}{\bar{F}_1(x)}. $$

Apply the mean value theorem and notice that $\bar{F}_2(h(x)) = \bar{F}_1(x)$,

$$ \frac{\bar{F}_2(\sigma x g_2(x) + h(x))}{\bar{F}_1(x)} = \frac{\bar{F}_2(h(x)) - p_2(\xi_x)\sigma x g_2(x)}{\bar{F}_2(h(x))} = 1 - \sigma x g_2(x) \frac{p_2(\xi_x)}{\bar{F}_2(h(x))} \to 1, $$

because $\sigma x g_2(x) \frac{p_2(\xi_x)}{\bar{F}_2(h(x))} \leq \sigma x g_2(x) \frac{p_2(h(x))}{\bar{F}_2(h(x))} \to 0, \quad \text{as } x \to +\infty$.

So, $\lim_{x \to +\infty} (1 - F_2(x)) / (1 - F_1(f^{-1}(x))) = 1$. $\square$
